What Goes Into a Strong Capability Statement

Contracting officers and prime contractors see hundreds of these. A good one tells a busy reader what you do and who has trusted you, in seconds. Here is how a construction or services firm should build one.

By Farier Consulting & Contracting · Updated July 2026

A capability statement is a one-page snapshot of your business, written for government buyers and the primes who team with them. Think of it as part company biography and part marketing document. Contracting officers request them during market research. Small-business specialists ask for them at matchmaking events. Prime contractors want them before they will consider a teaming arrangement. Without one, you are effectively invisible in the federal marketplace.

It often gets scanned into agency databases and pulled up later when a requirement appears, so it needs to be clear, clean and easy to read at a glance. Keep it to one page. Two at the very most, and only if you have extensive past performance.

The sections that matter

1. Core competencies

List four to six competencies that directly match the work you want, written as short, results-focused bullets. Be specific. "Construction services" is too vague to be useful. "Interior renovation and facility repair for occupied government buildings" tells a contracting officer exactly what you do. Use the language that appears in the solicitations you are targeting, because that is what buyers search for.

2. Past or relevant performance

Show a handful of relevant projects, not a wall of them. For each, name the client, the work you performed and the outcome. If you do not yet have federal past performance, that is not a dead end. Contracting officers understand that new entrants start somewhere, and relevant commercial or state and local work is acceptable evidence that you have done similar work before.

3. Differentiators

Explain briefly why a buyer should choose you over an equally qualified firm. Specialised experience, a strong safety or quality record, responsiveness, geographic coverage, or a particular type of facility you know well. This section is less important than past performance, so keep it tight and honest.

4. Company data

Put the practical details where a buyer can find them fast, usually along the bottom: legal business name, UEI, CAGE code, primary and secondary NAICS codes, any socioeconomic certifications you actually hold, and a named point of contact with a direct email and phone number. Do not claim certifications you have not been awarded.

The best capability statements work like a well-labeled toolbox. A buyer can see what is inside in seconds. If they have to hunt for the basics, they move on.

How it gets used

A capability statement is rarely the thing that wins the contract by itself. It is the thing that gets you the next conversation. It shows up in introduction emails, in follow-ups after industry days, as an appendix to proposals, and in teaming talks with primes. Buyers who find you through your SAM.gov profile will often ask for it as a quick read. Most of these exchanges will not turn into work, but none of them will if you do not have a strong statement ready.

Keep it current

Update your capability statement roughly every quarter, and tailor a version to the specific agency or opportunity whenever you can. Keep one master template current, then create targeted versions that lead with the competencies that match each pursuit.

A note for construction firms specifically

Construction and facility work is visual and concrete, so use that. Reference the types of facilities you have worked in, the trades you coordinate, your approach to safety and quality control, and how you document and close out a project. Buyers of construction services are buying reliability and low risk as much as capability, so let your statement signal both.
