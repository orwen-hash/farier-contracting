Prime and Subcontractor Teaming in Federal Construction

Most firms do not start out winning federal contracts as a prime. They start by earning a place on someone else's team. Here is how teaming works, and how a newer firm gets invited.

By Farier Consulting & Contracting · Updated July 2026

When the government awards a contract, the company that receives it is the prime contractor. The prime holds the award and carries responsibility to the agency for delivering the whole scope. To perform parts of that work, the prime often hires other companies. Each of those is a subcontractor. In construction, this is the normal shape of the work: one accountable prime coordinating a set of specialist and support firms underneath it.

Why teaming exists

Federal construction requirements are frequently too broad for any single small firm to cover alone. A solicitation might combine general construction, specific trades, facility systems, and management or documentation obligations. Teaming lets a prime assemble the full range of capability, and lets smaller firms contribute the pieces they do best without having to carry the entire award. It is also how many firms build the past performance they will later need to compete as a prime themselves.

For a newer company, teaming is not a consolation prize. It is the most direct route to real federal experience, references and relationships.

Where the capability statement comes in

Primes do not hand out subcontract roles casually. Before they include a firm on a bid, they want to know it is real, capable and low-risk to work with. The capability statement is the document they use to make that first judgment. When a prime is assembling a team, they will often collect statements from several candidate firms and shortlist from there. A clear, current statement that names your competencies, relevant work and company data is frequently what gets you into the conversation.

What primes are really evaluating

  • Fit. Do your competencies match the scope they need to cover?
  • Reliability. Will you show up, communicate and deliver without creating risk for the prime's relationship with the agency?
  • Documentation. Can you produce the quality-control records, reporting and closeout paperwork public work demands?
  • Registration. Are you set up to be paid and to sign a subcontract, with an active SAM.gov record and the identifiers in place?

How a newer firm earns a place

Lead with what you can honestly deliver, not with everything you might one day do. Present relevant commercial or local work clearly, even if you have no federal award yet. Make it easy for a prime to see the specific slice of scope you would own. And be the partner who is straightforward about capacity and timelines, because a prime is trusting you with a piece of their obligation to the government.

Practical step

Keep a one-page capability statement ready to send the moment a prime asks, and reach out before a solicitation drops rather than after. Relationships built early are the ones that turn into teaming agreements later.
